A. What burnout is and is not

R1

World Health Organization. (2019). Burn-out an "occupational phenomenon": International Classification of Diseases.

Classification statementTheory

Shows
ICD-11 describes burn-out as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: exhaustion, increased mental distance or negativism toward one's job, and reduced professional efficacy. It is classified as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.
Cannot establish
It is a definition, not a diagnostic instrument. It says nothing about prevalence, causes, or treatment, and it explicitly limits the term to the occupational context.

Open source ↗

R2

Maslach, C., & Leiter, M. P. (2016). Understanding the burnout experience: Recent research and its implications for psychiatry. World Psychiatry, 15(2), 103–111.

Narrative reviewAdjacent

Shows
Summarizes the three-dimension model (exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy) and the "areas of worklife" framework, in which burnout tends to follow a mismatch between the person and the job in six areas: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Also distinguishes burnout from depression at the conceptual level.
Cannot establish
Cross-occupational and written by the originators of the dominant model. It is a well-informed summary, not an independent test, and it does not report teacher-specific effect sizes.

Open source ↗

R3

Bianchi, R., Schonfeld, I. S., & Laurent, E. (2015). Burnout–depression overlap: A review. Clinical Psychology Review, 36, 28–41.

Systematic reviewAdjacent

Shows
Reviews 92 studies and finds that burnout and depressive symptoms overlap substantially, and that the empirical case for treating them as distinct conditions is weaker than often assumed. Useful as a corrective to the idea that "it's only burnout" rules out depression.
Cannot establish
The authors hold a contested position (that burnout may be a form of depression); Maslach and others disagree. The review does not resolve the question. It does not say that every burned-out person is depressed, only that the boundary is unclear and that persistent symptoms warrant clinical assessment.

B. Demands, control, and helplessness

R4

Karasek, R. A. (1979). Job demands, job decision latitude, and mental strain: Implications for job redesign. Administrative Science Quarterly, 24(2), 285–308.

Foundational empirical paperTheory

Shows
The original demand–control model: mental strain is highest not simply where demands are high, but where high demands combine with low decision latitude. The same workload is more harmful when the worker has little say over how it is met.
Cannot establish
Based on Swedish and American worker surveys from the 1970s, not teachers. It is included because it is the intellectual root of the claim that control matters independently of load.

Open source ↗

R5

Bakker, A. B., Demerouti, E., & Sanz-Vergel, A. (2023). Job demands–resources theory: Ten years later. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 10, 25–53.

Theoretical reviewTheory

Shows
The current state of the job demands–resources (JD-R) model: demands (workload, emotional demands, conflict) drive exhaustion through a health-impairment path, while resources (autonomy, support, feedback) drive engagement and buffer the effect of demands. Reviews the evidence that resources matter most when demands are high.
Cannot establish
A general model across occupations. Teacher-specific tests of it are in Section C.

Open source ↗

R6

Maier, S. F., & Seligman, M. E. P. (2016). Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience. Psychological Review, 123(4), 349–367.

Theory and neuroscience reviewTheory

Shows
Revises the original learned-helplessness account. Passivity in the face of prolonged uncontrollable stress is the default response; what organisms learn is control, and that learning dampens the stress response. The practical implication is that experiences of control are protective and can be acquired.
Cannot establish
Mechanistic evidence comes mainly from animal experiments and human laboratory studies. It offers a way to think about the difference between a job that is hard and a job in which nothing one does seems to matter, not a measured finding about teachers.

C. What drives burnout in teaching

R7

Aloe, A. M., Shisler, S. M., Norris, B. D., Nickerson, A. B., & Rinker, T. W. (2014). A multivariate meta-analysis of student misbehavior and teacher burnout. Educational Research Review, 12, 30–44.

Meta-analysis, 21 samplesDirect

Shows
Student misbehavior is significantly associated with all three burnout dimensions, most strongly with emotional exhaustion. This is the most rigorous pooled estimate available for the behavior–burnout link.
Cannot establish
The underlying studies are correlational and mostly cross-sectional. The association runs both ways in principle: exhausted teachers may also manage classrooms less effectively.

Open source ↗

R8

Collie, R. J., Shapka, J. D., & Perry, N. E. (2012). School climate and social–emotional learning: Predicting teacher stress, job satisfaction, and teaching efficacy. Journal of Educational Psychology, 104(4), 1189–1204.

Survey, 664 teachersDirect

Shows
Of four school-climate factors, teachers' perceptions of student motivation and behavior had the strongest relationship with stress, teaching efficacy, and job satisfaction. Stress from student behavior was associated with lower sense of efficacy.
Cannot establish
Cross-sectional self-report from Canadian elementary and secondary teachers. Shows which climate factors travel with stress, not which cause it.

Open source ↗

R9

Hakanen, J. J., Bakker, A. B., & Schaufeli, W. B. (2006). Burnout and work engagement among teachers. Journal of School Psychology, 43(6), 495–513.

Survey, 2,038 teachersDirect

Shows
A test of the JD-R model in teachers: job demands predicted burnout and ill health, while job resources (including control and supervisory support) predicted engagement and commitment. Burnout partly mediated the path from demands to poor health.
Cannot establish
Cross-sectional, Finnish sample. The demand and resource measures are broad, so it supports the shape of the model rather than any one specific lever.

Open source ↗

R10

Skaalvik, E. M., & Skaalvik, S. (2014). Teacher self-efficacy and perceived autonomy: Relations with teacher engagement, job satisfaction, and emotional exhaustion. Psychological Reports, 114(1), 68–77.

SurveyDirect

Shows
Perceived autonomy was positively related to engagement and job satisfaction and negatively related to emotional exhaustion, with self-efficacy playing a parallel role. One of the few studies that isolates autonomy as a variable in teachers rather than folding it into "resources."
Cannot establish
Cross-sectional, Norwegian sample. Autonomy here is self-reported perception, not an audit of what teachers were actually permitted to decide.

Open source ↗

R11

Skaalvik, E. M., & Skaalvik, S. (2021). Teacher burnout: Relations between dimensions of burnout, perceived school context, job satisfaction and motivation for teaching. A longitudinal study. Teachers and Teaching, 27(7), 602–616.

Longitudinal survey, 262 teachersDirect

Shows
Demands and resources measured in September predicted burnout dimensions in April, and different stressors mapped to different dimensions: time pressure was the strongest predictor of exhaustion, while low student motivation and working in a context that conflicts with one's values were the strongest predictors of cynicism. Autonomy was positively associated with sense of accomplishment.
Cannot establish
Two time points within one school year, Norwegian high school teachers. Stronger than cross-sectional work on direction of effect, but still not experimental.

Open source ↗

R12

Madigan, D. J., & Kim, L. E. (2021). Towards an understanding of teacher attrition: A meta-analysis of burnout, job satisfaction, and teachers' intentions to quit. Teaching and Teacher Education, 105, 103425.

Meta-analysisDirect

Shows
All three burnout dimensions were positively related to intention to quit, exhaustion most strongly (r ≈ .41). Burnout and job satisfaction together explained about 27% of the variance in intentions to quit, and the burnout–quit relationship appeared to have grown stronger over the decades covered.
Cannot establish
Intentions, not actual departures. Pooled from 24 studies with varying measures; the "increasing over time" finding rests on study publication year as a moderator and should be read cautiously.

Open source ↗

R13

Steiner, E. D., Levine, P. R., & Doan, S. (2026). Teacher well-being, pay, and intentions to leave in 2026: Selected findings from the 2026 State of the American Teacher survey. RAND Corporation, RR-A4404-2.

Nationally representative surveyDirect

Shows
In 2026, 55% of U.S. K–12 public school teachers reported frequent job-related stress (down from 62% in 2025) against 34% of similar working adults, and teachers were more likely to report burnout, difficulty coping, and symptoms of depression on every indicator, a pattern consistent since 2021. Managing student behavior was the most frequently named source of stress. The companion technical report (RR-A4404-3) holds the full tables.
Cannot establish
Descriptive prevalence, not causation. Funded by the NEA and AFT, which the report discloses; the survey panel and weighting are documented in the technical report and the findings align with independent surveys.

D. Mistreatment and institutional response

R14

McMahon, S. D., Reaves, S., McConnell, E. A., Peist, E., Ruiz, L., & APA Task Force on Classroom Violence Directed Against Teachers. (2017). The ecology of teachers' experiences with violence and lack of administrative support. American Journal of Community Psychology, 60(3–4), 502–515.

Qualitative analysis of survey narrativesDirect

Shows
Analyzes teachers' written accounts of violent incidents in which they felt unsupported. Recurring themes include administrators minimizing or dismissing incidents, placing responsibility on the teacher, failing to follow through on discipline, and leaving the teacher uncertain whether reporting was worth the cost. This is the closest direct evidence for the "institutional response" concern in this resource.
Cannot establish
The sample is teachers who chose to describe unsupportive responses, so it documents what the experience looks like, not how common it is. It reports the teacher's perspective only.

Open source ↗

R15

McMahon, S. D., Worrell, F. C., Reddy, L. A., Martinez, A., Espelage, D. L., Astor, R. A., Anderman, E. M., Valido, A., Swenski, T., Perry, A. H., Dudek, C. M., & Bare, K. (2024). Violence and aggression against educators and school personnel, retention, stress, and training needs: National survey results. American Psychologist.

National survey, ~12,000 respondentsDirect

Shows
After pandemic restrictions lifted, verbal and threatening aggression against teachers returned to or exceeded pre-pandemic levels (roughly four in five teachers in 2022), with aggression coming from students, parents, colleagues, and administrators. Experiencing aggression was associated with anxiety and stress and, through them, with intentions to transfer or leave. The task force recommends administrative support and training over zero-tolerance discipline.
Cannot establish
Convenience sample; the authors note that people who had experienced violence may have been more likely to respond, so the percentages are likely upper bounds. Cross-sectional.

Open source ↗

R16

Smith, C. P., & Freyd, J. J. (2014). Institutional betrayal. American Psychologist, 69(6), 575–587.

Conceptual paper with supporting studiesAdjacent

Shows
Defines institutional betrayal as a trusted institution acting, or failing to act, in ways that harm those who depend on it, and reviews evidence that an unsupportive institutional response worsens outcomes after a harmful event beyond the harm of the event itself. Identifies institutional features that tend to precede betrayal, such as prioritizing reputation and lacking clear reporting procedures.
Cannot establish
Developed primarily in the context of sexual assault on campuses and in the military. Applying it to a school's response to a teacher's complaint is an extension by analogy, not a tested application.

Open source ↗

E. Moral distress, demoralization, and moral injury

R17

Morley, G., Ives, J., Bradbury-Jones, C., & Irvine, F. (2019). What is "moral distress"? A narrative synthesis of the literature. Nursing Ethics, 26(3), 646–662.

Systematic review and narrative synthesis, 34 papersAdjacent

Shows
Traces the concept from Jameton's 1984 definition (knowing the right thing to do but being constrained from doing it) through later broadenings, and proposes that moral distress requires three things together: a moral event, psychological distress, and a causal link between them. Useful for keeping the term precise.
Cannot establish
Entirely from nursing and health-care ethics. Whether the construct behaves the same way in teaching has not been systematically tested.

Open source ↗

R18

Litz, B. T., Stein, N., Delaney, E., Lebowitz, L., Nash, W. P., Silva, C., & Maguen, S. (2009). Moral injury and moral repair in war veterans: A preliminary model and intervention strategy. Clinical Psychology Review, 29(8), 695–706.

Conceptual modelAdjacent

Shows
The source of the term "moral injury" as now used: lasting psychological harm from perpetrating, failing to prevent, or witnessing acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs. Included so that the resource can distinguish moral injury from moral distress rather than use them interchangeably.
Cannot establish
A model built for combat veterans. Its use for teachers is metaphorical unless and until validated; this resource does not apply it to educators.

Open source ↗

R19

Santoro, D. A. (2018). Demoralized: Why teachers leave the profession they love and how they can stay. Harvard Education Press.

Interview-based philosophical study, 23 teachersDirect

Shows
Argues that much of what gets called burnout in experienced teachers is better described as demoralization: the teacher still has energy and commitment but is repeatedly prevented by policy, mandate, or institutional norm from doing what they believe good teaching requires. Because the cause is a value conflict rather than depletion, self-care does not resolve it; Santoro describes "re-moralization" through professional community, voice, and principled action.
Cannot establish
Interpretive work with a small, self-selected sample of experienced teachers who stayed. It cannot say how prevalent demoralization is, and its distinction from burnout is conceptual rather than psychometrically tested.

F. What helps

R20

Iancu, A. E., Rusu, A., Măroiu, C., Păcurar, R., & Maricuțoiu, L. P. (2018). The effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing teacher burnout: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 30, 373–396.

Meta-analysis of controlled interventionsDirect

Shows
Interventions aimed at teachers (mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral, psychoeducation, social-emotional skills, professional development) produced small but significant reductions in overall burnout, emotional exhaustion, and reduced accomplishment. Interventions shorter than a month had smaller effects. Most studies were rated good quality.
Cannot establish
Nearly all interventions reviewed were directed at the individual teacher. The meta-analysis therefore says that teacher-directed programs help modestly; it cannot say how they compare with changing the job itself, because almost no controlled studies in teaching have tried that.

Open source ↗

R21

Panagioti, M., Panagopoulou, E., Bower, P., Lewith, G., Kontopantelis, E., Chew-Graham, C., Dawson, S., van Marwijk, H., Geraghty, K., & Esmail, A. (2017). Controlled interventions to reduce burnout in physicians: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Internal Medicine, 177(2), 195–205.

Meta-analysis, 20 controlled studiesAdjacent

Shows
Across 1,550 physicians, interventions reduced burnout by a small amount overall, but organization-directed interventions (schedule changes, workload reduction, more participation in decisions) had roughly two and a half times the effect of individual-directed ones (SMD −0.45 versus −0.18). The authors conclude that burnout is a problem of the organization rather than of individuals.
Cannot establish
Physicians, not teachers. It is the best available controlled evidence on the individual-versus-organizational question and the finding is consistent with the JD-R research on teachers, but it has not been replicated in schools.

Open source ↗

What is still missing. No study in this set tests whether fear of complaints or liability changes how teachers act, and none measures institutional betrayal in a school workforce. Those remain open questions, and the reflection page is written to reflect that.
