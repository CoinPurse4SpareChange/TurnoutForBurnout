Moral distress and institutional trust

When an educator’s professional obligations collide with constraints on what they believe they can responsibly do.

What is moral distress?

Moral distress is a concept developed prominently in nursing ethics: a person perceives an ethically appropriate course of action but encounters constraints that prevent it. Its application to schooling is promising but should not automatically be treated as a clinically established syndrome among teachers.

A classroom example

A teacher believes a disruptive situation requires a specific, proportionate safety intervention. The teacher also believes a complaint could result in disciplinary scrutiny or job consequences. Both beliefs should be investigated: the intervention may or may not be appropriate, and the perceived restriction may or may not reflect actual policy or law.

Moral distress is not moral injury

Moral distress concerns constrained ethical action. Moral injury is often used for deeper psychological consequences connected to perceived violations of fundamental moral commitments; definitions and measurement vary across settings. Institutional betrayal focuses on how a trusted organization responds to harmful experiences. These concepts may overlap but should not be collapsed.

Why response matters

When an incident occurs, a fair investigation, clear procedural support, and transparent explanation can matter to both students and educators. Our proposed research model asks whether perceived institutional abandonment magnifies the effects of mistreatment.

Evidence status: The integrated pathway linking student mistreatment, fear of liability, moral distress, and subsequent depression is a research hypothesis—not an empirically established universal sequence. See our methods page.
