Start here

For the educator who has begun asking: Why am I no longer responding to work—or the people in my life—the way I used to?

Imagine a teacher who has always been patient, thoughtful, and quick to de-escalate. After repeated incidents of disrespect and uncertainty about whether administrators will support reasonable boundaries, the teacher becomes guarded, impatient, and exhausted.

That shift does not prove a specific diagnosis or establish that the workplace is the sole cause. But it is a reason to ask about the environment—not merely the teacher’s resilience.

You can be responsible for how you treat others without being solely responsible for the conditions contributing to your distress.

Three questions worth asking

  • What outcomes am I expected to deliver, and which decisions do I actually control?
  • What happens when I report mistreatment or ask for support?
  • Are my symptoms limited to work, or have they begun to affect my sleep, relationships, and life outside school?

Research links student misbehavior with teacher burnout [R1], and organizational psychology describes how demanding work interacts with resources such as autonomy and support [R3].

If emotional or depressive symptoms persist or worsen, qualified mental health support can be valuable even when workplace conditions contribute to them. Workplace reform and individual care are complementary—not competing—responses.
