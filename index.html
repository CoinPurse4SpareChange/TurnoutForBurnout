Are the conditions you are working in right now sustainable for you, right now?

A short walk through what the research says wears teachers down, with a way to take stock of your own situation as you go. It is not a test and it does not diagnose anything. It is a structured way to notice what you are carrying, and to see it laid out in one place.

How this page works

Read it from top to bottom. Each section states something the research has found, shows the evidence, and then lists a few plain statements about working conditions. Mark the ones that are true for you right now. "Right now" matters: conditions change, and so does your capacity to carry them, so the same page can read differently next semester.

The first part is about the job. The second, shorter part is about what the job is currently costing you. At the end the page shows both side by side, because the question that matters is not how much is wrong with the job or how tired you are, but whether the two together are sustainable for you at this moment.

Nothing you mark leaves this page. Close it and it is gone. If you would rather work on paper, print it; the boxes work either way.

Every number in brackets links to the research library, where each source has a plain note on what it shows and what it cannot establish.

Part oneThe conditions

Teaching has become measurably harder, and that is not a feeling.

In 2026, 55 percent of U.S. public school teachers reported frequent job-related stress, against 34 percent of similar working adults. Teachers were more likely than those adults to report burnout, difficulty coping, and symptoms of depression, and the gap has held every year since the survey began in 2021. [R13] Roughly four in five teachers surveyed in 2022 had experienced verbal or threatening aggression at work in the past year. [R15] Managing student behavior is the stressor teachers name most often [R13], and student misbehavior is associated with all three dimensions of burnout, most strongly with exhaustion. [R7] [R8]

If the job feels harder than it used to, that is consistent with what the measurements show. The sections that follow break "harder" into the specific conditions the research has tied to burnout, so you can see which of them are present where you work.

Time pressure is the condition most strongly tied to exhaustion.

In a study that measured working conditions in the autumn and burnout the following spring, time pressure was the strongest predictor of emotional exhaustion, ahead of student behavior and every other stressor measured. [R11] Across occupations, job demands drive exhaustion through what researchers call the health-impairment path. [R5] In 2026, thirty percent of teachers held a job outside their school system. [R13]

True for me right now

The same workload is harder to carry when you have little say over how it is met.

This is the oldest finding in occupational health psychology. Strain is highest not where demands are high, but where high demands combine with low decision latitude; a heavy load with real discretion is far less harmful than the same load without it. [R4] Among teachers, perceived autonomy goes with higher engagement and lower exhaustion [R10], and autonomy measured in the autumn predicted a stronger sense of accomplishment in the spring. [R11]

Autonomy here does not mean freedom from oversight. It means meaningful discretion within the rules: how to pace a unit, how to respond to a disruption in the moment, when to handle something yourself and when to escalate.

True for me right now

What happens after an incident can matter as much as the incident.

When teachers describe incidents of mistreatment in which they felt unsupported afterward, the same themes recur: the incident was minimized or dismissed, responsibility was placed on the teacher, promised consequences did not follow, and the teacher was left unsure whether reporting the next one would be worth it. [R14] Research on institutional betrayal, from other settings, finds that an unsupportive response from a trusted institution worsens outcomes beyond the harm of the event itself. [R16] The task force that studies violence against educators recommends administrative support and training, not zero-tolerance discipline, which the evidence does not support. [R15]

Fear of consequences for setting a boundary deserves one specific question: is the fear grounded in written policy, a management instruction, something that happened to a colleague, or a story that circulates? A clear answer, even an unwelcome one, is more workable than a standing uncertainty.

True for me right now

Being required to work against your own sense of good teaching feeds cynicism specifically.

In the longitudinal study, working in a setting whose values conflicted with the teacher's own was one of the two strongest predictors of cynicism, alongside low student motivation. [R11] The dominant model of burnout names a values mismatch as one of six conditions under which it develops. [R2] Interviews with experienced teachers suggest that much of what gets called burnout in veterans is better described as demoralization: the teacher still has energy and commitment but is repeatedly prevented from doing what they believe good teaching requires, and rest does not touch that. [R19] The nursing literature has a precise name for the general case, moral distress: knowing the right thing to do and being constrained from doing it. [R17] That is a different thing from moral injury, a term from research on veterans that this page does not apply to teachers. [R18]

True for me right now

Recognition and fairness are part of the picture, not extras.

Reward, community, and fairness are three of the six areas in which a mismatch between a person and a job predicts burnout. [R2] Social support from supervisors and colleagues is one of the resources that most reliably buffers the effect of demands. [R5] [R9]

True for me right now

Conditions like these, sustained, produce a predictable response.

The learned-helplessness research is the best-known account of what happens when actions repeatedly fail to change outcomes. Its originators revised the theory in 2016 in a way that matters here: passivity in the face of prolonged uncontrollable stress is not something a person has to learn. It is the default. What gets learned, when it does, is control, the discovery that one's actions matter, and that discovery is what restores active coping. [R6]

This is theory drawn largely from laboratory work, not a measured finding about teachers. But it reframes something people tend to hold against themselves. Having stopped trying after a long stretch in which trying changed nothing is the expected response to that situation. It is also, in this account, reversible, because what is missing is the experience of efficacy rather than the capacity for it.

One distinction worth keeping. A teacher who has concluded that nothing they do will change a situation may be entirely right about that situation. In that case the problem is the situation, not the belief, and the response is to change what can be changed or to leave what cannot. Nothing on this page assumes the belief is mistaken.

Part twoWhat it is costing you right now

Burnout is a description of a person in a job, not a verdict on the person.

The World Health Organization describes burnout as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three dimensions: exhaustion; increased mental distance from one's job, or cynicism about it; and reduced professional efficacy. It is classified as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. [R1] Burnout is the strongest known predictor of a teacher's intention to leave, and the research does not treat leaving as a failure; it treats it as the predictable outcome of a mismatch between a person and a job. [R12]

The three dimensions are worth keeping separate, because the conditions in part one feed them differently: time pressure tends to produce exhaustion, value conflict and unresponsive students tend to produce distance, and lack of autonomy tends to erode the sense of being effective. [R11] The statements below are plain-language descriptions of each dimension. They are not a clinical scale.

Part threeWhat the evidence says helps

Two findings that belong together.

Programs aimed at the individual teacher (mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral training, psychoeducation, social-emotional skills, classroom-management training) produce small but reliable reductions in burnout, and longer programs do more than short ones. [R20] The best controlled evidence on what helps more comes from physicians rather than teachers: there, interventions that changed the job (schedules, workload, participation in decisions) had roughly two and a half times the effect of interventions that trained the person. [R21] That comparison has not been replicated in schools, but it is consistent with everything the teacher research says about where burnout comes from. [R9] [R11]

If you have tried self-care and it has not fixed things, that is the expected result. Individual approaches are worth doing, and they are not a remedy for conditions that are structured to exhaust people. The larger levers are mostly properties of the job. That is a fact about the problem, and it is also a reason to set down a particular kind of self-blame.

What is partly within reach.

The evidence does not prescribe any of these. It identifies the conditions they address, and some of those conditions can be approached from where you stand even when they cannot be changed outright.

  • One domain of real control. If what restores active coping is the experience that your actions matter, the domain does not need to be large. [R6] An area of your work where your decisions clearly produce results, protected, is not a consolation prize in that account.
  • Time pressure. It was the strongest predictor of exhaustion. [R11] Any room to negotiate preparation load, number of distinct courses, or non-teaching duties is room aimed at the right dimension.
  • People who see it the way you do. Teachers who recovered from demoralization did so largely by finding colleagues who shared their sense of what good teaching is, and by voicing the conflict rather than carrying it alone. [R19] Support is one of the resources the research most consistently finds protective. [R5]
  • Clarity about constraints. Finding out whether a feared consequence rests on written policy or on rumor converts an open uncertainty into a known fact, and known facts can be planned around.
  • A different context. Because burnout is a property of the fit between a person and a job rather than of the person, the same teacher in a different school, role, or district can have a different experience. [R12] Moving is not the only option and is not always available, but considering it is not giving up.

Where burnout overlaps with something else.

Burnout and depression overlap, and the research has not cleanly separated them. [R3] The practical implication does not depend on how that debate resolves. If what you are experiencing has stayed within the boundaries of work, a workplace explanation is likely sufficient. If it has spread into sleep, relationships, and the capacity to enjoy anything, or if it includes persistent hopelessness, a workplace explanation may still be true and is no longer sufficient on its own. The job can be the cause and talking to a clinician can still be the right response. Working to change the conditions and getting care for yourself are complementary, and neither is a concession about the other.

What this page does not say. It does not say what you should do. It says what the research has found, how strong the findings are, and which conditions they point at. It does not assume every workplace is harmful or every limit on a teacher's authority is wrong; students have rights, educators have rights, and both deserve careful protection. The decisions are yours, and they depend on facts about your situation that no study can supply.
